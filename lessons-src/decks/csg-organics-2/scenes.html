Copal is young, not fully fossilised resin: softer, and it turns sticky with a drop of acetone.
Amber treatments and how to spot them

Oiling and heating

Clarifies cloudy amber; heating can create disc-shaped “sun spangle” stress cracks.

Dyeing

Adds colour; it concentrates in cracks.

Reconstruction · ambroid

Small pieces pressed together with heat. Look for flow lines, elongated bubbles and sharp clear-and-cloudy boundaries. Insects may be added to fake value.

Hot-point test (on a hidden spot): amber gives a piney, resinous smell; plastic gives an acrid, chemical smell. In saturated salt water amber and copal float, most plastics and glass sink.

Ammolite · iridescent fossil ammonite shell

What it is

The aragonite shell of ammonites, sea creatures that died out with the dinosaurs at the end of the Mesozoic.

Where

Mainly the eastern slopes of the Rocky Mountains, Alberta, Canada.

Ammolite is thin and fragile, so it is often sold as doublets or triplets with a quartz or spinel cap.
Ivory and jet

Ivory

Elephant tusk is the classic source; also walrus, hippo, sperm whale, narwhal and boar. White to yellow, yellows with age, greasy to dull lustre. Elephant ivory shows engine turning. Trade is heavily restricted under CITES.

Jet

A compact form of lignite coal from driftwood buried in sea-floor mud. Black, high polish, very light; burns like coal. Classic source: Whitby, England. Imitations: glass (“Paris jet”), vulcanite, plastic, dyed chalcedony.

Shell and tortoiseshell

Shell

Cut as cameos, beads, cabochons and inlays. Shells with an iridescent orient are sold as mother-of-pearl.

Tortoiseshell

Shell of the hawksbill sea turtle, now protected. Mottled amber, brown and black; smells of burning hair. Under magnification: random tiny dots. Plastic imitations show regular patterns and mould lines.
