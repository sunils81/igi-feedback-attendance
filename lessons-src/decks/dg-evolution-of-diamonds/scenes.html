Why diamonds?

Why are diamonds so expensive, and why are they loved by everyone?

Three reasons

Beauty

Durability

Rarity

Many gems have one or two of these. Diamond has all three, and that is the whole story of its value.

Beauty · what light does inside a diamond

Four ways a diamond plays with light

Brilliance

The white light reflected back to your eye.

Dispersion

White light split into spectral colours, also called fire.

Scintillation

The sparkle: flashes of light as the stone, light or viewer moves.

Lustre

The surface shine. Diamond’s is called adamantine, the highest of any gem.

Bars show absolute hardness (sclerometer). The Mohs scale only ranks minerals; it is not linear.

Diamond is the hardest natural substance on the planet, roughly four times harder than corundum (ruby and sapphire), the next mineral on the scale.
Hardness is why diamonds keep crisp, sharp facet edges for generations, while softer gems slowly round off.
2 · Toughness
The four cleavage directions follow the 8 facesDrag to turn

Tough, with four weak directions

A diamond can take a lot of everyday wear and tear. But it has four planes of weakness, called cleavage planes, running parallel to the faces of the octahedron.

A sharp knock in exactly the wrong direction can split a diamond along a cleavage plane. Hard does not mean unbreakable.
Cutters use these same planes on purpose: the cleaver splits rough along them (Section 05).
3 · Chemical stability

Every carbon atom holds four neighbours

Diamond is pure carbon. Each carbon atom shares a strong covalent bond with four neighbouring carbon atoms, building one rigid three-dimensional network.

Those bonds make diamond chemically very stable: everyday chemicals, perfumes and cleaning products cannot harm it.
Crystallography in one picture

Atoms can be arranged at random, or in perfect order

Amorphous · random

Amorphous

Atoms are scattered at random, like glass. No crystal faces, no fixed shape.

Crystalline

Atoms repeat in a regular three-dimensional pattern, the crystal lattice. Diamond is crystalline, which is why it grows with flat faces and a predictable shape.

This order is what we study in crystallography, and it is why diamond rough has recognisable shapes (Day 2: morphology).
Durability, in one line

Diamonds are forever, and for everyone.

Hard enough to keep its polish, tough enough for daily wear, stable enough to be handed down. That is why a diamond can carry a promise.

Are diamonds really rare, or made rare?

Diamonds are found in abundance in smaller sizes and lower colours and clarities.

Stones of 1 carat and above, with high colour and high clarity, are genuinely rare. Section 02 shows why nature makes so few of them.

Born in the deep Earth

Formed deep within the Earth, billions of years ago, by intense heat and pressure.

Where diamonds are made

Deep, hot, high-pressure, and very old

DepthAbout 150–250 km, far deeper than ordinary volcanoes reachHeatRoughly 900–1,300 °CPressureAround 45,000 times the air pressure at sea levelAgeMost are 1 to 3.5 billion years old
A diamond needs just the right conditions for a very long time, then a fast ride to the surface. Large, clean crystals need everything to go right, which is why they are so rare.
The volcanic pipeline

Diamonds ride to the surface in rare, deep eruptions

The eruption is fast. If the magma rose slowly, the diamonds would turn to graphite or dissolve on the way up. The carrot-shaped pipes left behind are where primary mines dig today.
The host rocks

Kimberlite and lamproite

These two volcanic rocks carry diamonds up from the mantle. Kimberlite is named after Kimberley in South Africa.

Look closely: the crystals in the rock are octahedra, exactly the shape we study on Day 2.

Mining

Diamonds are mined in two ways: from the pipe where they arrived, or from wherever rivers and the sea carried them later.

Two kinds of deposit
Primary

In the host rock

Diamonds are dug out of the kimberlite or lamproite pipe itself, by open-pit and then underground mining.

Secondary

Carried away by nature

Over millions of years the pipes erode. Rivers and the sea carry diamonds into alluvial, beach and marine deposits.

Primary mining · underground

When the pit gets too deep, mining goes underground

Shafts and tunnels are driven below the pit to reach the rest of the pipe, sometimes more than a kilometre down.

Mining in the Arctic · Canada

Ekati and the ice road

The Lac de Gras mines sit on frozen tundra with no permanent road. Heavy supplies come in over a winter ice road, built across frozen lakes and open for only a few weeks each year.

Secondary mining · alluvial deposits

Diamonds among river gravels

Rivers wash diamonds out of eroded pipes and drop them with heavy gravels, sometimes hundreds of kilometres away.

Wet and dry digging
Marine deposits · Namibia

Mining the beach, below sea level

Sea walls hold back the Atlantic while machines strip away sand to reach the diamond-bearing gravels on the bedrock.

An important trade fact

Rough from secondary deposits is usually of higher quality

The journey down rivers and along beaches is rough on rough. Crystals with cracks and heavy inclusions break up on the way.

What survives is mostly the tougher, cleaner crystals: nature has already done the first sort.

Mine to market

Every parcel of rough is certified, sorted and planned before a single facet is polished.

Kimberley Process certificate
Kimberley Process

Certificate for rough diamonds

Conflict-
free
certified

Every border crossing, every parcel

Since 2003, the Kimberley Process requires every shipment of rough diamonds crossing a border to travel in a sealed container with a government certificate.

Its purpose: keep “conflict diamonds”, rough sold to fund wars against governments, out of the legitimate trade.
What arrives at the sorting table

Every crystal is different in shape, size, colour and clarity. Sorting them is Day 4; reading their shapes is Day 2.

The last step before grading

Boiling

After polishing, the diamond is boiled in a mixture of sulphuric and nitric acids to remove any surface residue, such as polishing dust and oil.

Coloured matter in inclusions that reach the surface can also be bleached by the boiling, which can change how the stone looks under the loupe.
What we learned today

Why diamonds

Beauty (brilliance, fire, scintillation, lustre), durability (hardness, toughness, chemical stability) and rarity.

Formation

Deep in the mantle, 1–3.5 billion years ago, brought up fast in kimberlite and lamproite pipes.

Mining

Primary (open pit, underground) and secondary (alluvial, marine). Extraction ends with X-ray and hand sorting.

Mine to market

Kimberley Process certificates, then planner → marker → cleaver → bruter → blocker → brillianteer, then boiling.
