Section 01

Where was it mined?

Buyers, governments and customers all ask. For coloured stones the answer is often inside the stone. For diamonds it is much harder.

Coloured stones · read the inclusions

Inclusions are a coloured stone’s fingerprint

Each deposit forms in its own chemistry, so the inclusions it traps can point to a country, sometimes even to a mine.

Three-phase inclusion in emerald

A jagged cavity holding liquid, a gas bubble and a tiny cube of salt. Classic for Colombian emerald.

Similar-looking inclusions elsewhere

Three-phase-like inclusions also occur in emeralds from Brazil and Australia, so one clue is never the whole answer.

Diamonds · a different story

Two ways we might trace a diamond

✕ Does not work

Studying inclusions

Diamonds from very different mines share the same mineral inclusions: garnet, olivine, diopside, sulphides. They grew in similar rock deep in the mantle, so inclusions don’t identify a source.

✓ Works, for parcels

Studying crystal morphology

Each mine produces a typical mixture of shapes, surfaces, colours and qualities. Read a whole parcel and the mixture points to the mine.

The Rough Diamond Classification Scheme

Developed by Dr Jeff Harris for De Beers, 1975

  • Based on the study of run-of-mine production: everything the mine recovers, not a hand-picked selection.
  • At least 1,000 stones must be studied for a reliable result.
  • Classifies rough into 5 primary and 12 secondary subdivisions of shape, surface and quality.
  • It cannot be used on an individual stone.
One stone tells you about that stone. A thousand stones tell you about the mine.
Think about it

A perfect octahedron. Where was it mined?

Mir, Russia?
Sierra Leone?
Wesselton, South Africa?
Ekati, Canada?
Any of them. Sharp octahedra are found in all four. A single crystal cannot be traced to its mine by looking at it.

D.R. Congo · Mbuji Mayi and Tshikapa

Home of the “Congo cubes”

Mbuji Mayi

About 95% low-quality bort. All models, with relatively many cubes. A large share of heavily coated stones and many combination forms: the “Congo rounds”.

Tshikapa

Much higher gem share (about 40%), like neighbouring Angola, but only about 5% of Congo’s total production.

Congo · surface features

Heavy coatings, skeleton growth and twinning

Angola, Canada and Botswana
Angola · 70% gem

Lucapa and Cuango

Lucapa: mainly octahedral, small cracks in the points, some frosted.

Cuango: mainly dodecahedral, many half-moon cracks, a large share frosted.

Full range of colours. Watch out: Angolan and Congolese goods look alike.

Canada

Ekati and Diavik

Comparable to Russian diamonds. Occasionally pinkish.

Botswana

Orapa and Jwaneng

Orapa: all models including cubes; equal piqué and fractured; full colour range, more yellow.

Jwaneng: all models with a large number of cubes; equal piqué and fractured; a number of stones with a green skin.

South Africa

Pipes, rivers and one unusual alluvial

Primary deposits (pipes)

All models and colours; a large content of smaller sizes; no cubes. Kimberley gives more “Cape” (yellowish) stones; Premier (Cullinan) more browns, and also light pink and light blue.

Secondary deposits (alluvial)

More rounded dodecahedra with heavy abrasion from river transport.

Dreyerspan

Mainly octahedral, which is unusual for an alluvial deposit. About 30% show a noticeable skin.

Helam, Transvaal

Irregular, often unclear

  • A large share of very irregular shapes
  • Light green coating on about 30%
  • Transparent and black cubes (2–3%)
  • “Amber” yellow and black irregular stones (2%)
  • No macles
Guinea, Sierra Leone, Central Africa and Brazil

Guinea

Shiny, smooth-edged goods. Octahedra usually white, dodecahedra usually green-blue. High brown content; some fancy colours.

Sierra Leone

Crystals with good surfaces. Fine coated diamonds.

Central Africa

Well-shaped crystals, good surfaces, mostly dodecahedra. Fine light-green coated stones; many colours including brown and yellow.

Brazil

Mostly smaller sizes but good quality. Known for carbonado and ballas. Colours include blue, white, yellow and red.

What we learned

Inclusions don’t trace diamonds

They work for coloured stones; diamonds from different mines share the same inclusions.

Parcels, not single stones

The Harris scheme reads at least 1,000 run-of-mine stones: 5 primary and 12 secondary subdivisions.

Each mine has a signature

Argyle: brown, pitted. Namibia: rounded, 80% gem. Congo: cubes and coatings. Udachnaya: layered octahedra.
