Section 01

Morphology

Before a rough diamond is ever weighed, sorted or planned, someone reads its shape. The shape tells you how the crystal grew, and what happened to it on the way up.

Definition

The study of minerals or crystals according to their outer shape.

1

How it grew

Each shape points to the conditions deep in the Earth where the crystal formed.

2

What happened next

Rounded edges, etch marks and skins record its journey to the surface.

3

What it can become

Sorters and planners use the shape to judge yield, sawing direction and value.

Layer upon layer

Natural diamonds grow in layers, outwards from a tiny seed

This layered, or “epitaxial”, growth adds one thin shell of carbon atoms after another, each copying the crystal structure underneath it.

The layers stay inside the stone for ever. After polishing they can appear as graining, faint lines that follow the old growth faces.

Section 02 · Monocrystals

Growth forms

One crystal, grown undisturbed. Diamond belongs to the cubic crystal system, so its natural growth shapes are built from the same symmetry: the octahedron and the cube.

Growth form 1 · the most common
OctahedronDrag to turn

The octahedron

Shape
8 triangular faces, 6 corners, 12 edges

Two four-sided pyramids joined base to base. Gem-quality octahedra are the classic “sawable” rough you will meet in sorting.

An exception

The pseudo-tetrahedron

Occasionally an octahedron grows with four of its faces much larger than the other four. The result looks like a tetrahedron (4 triangular faces), but it is still an octahedron underneath.

“Pseudo” means it only looks like a tetrahedron. Diamond’s true symmetry is still octahedral.

What it looks like: a tetrahedron

Growth form 2
CubeDrag to turn

The cube, or hexahedron

Shape
6 square faces

Natural cubes are much rarer than octahedra. Their faces are often rough or fibrous and many carry a coating, so most cubes end up as lower-quality or industrial goods.

Section 03

Dissolution

The journey to the surface is not gentle. Hot magma eats into the crystal, rounding its edges and corners. Some crystals also grow with two kinds of faces at once.

Watch the shape change
OctahedronDrag to turn

Dissolution forms

Corners and edges dissolve fastest, so the flat faces give way to curved, rounded ones.

Octahedron →dodecahedron, 12 rhombus-shaped facesCube →tetrakis hexahedron, 24 triangular faces

Press each shape in turn and watch the model change.

In the rough parcel

Rounded dodecahedra: the dissolution forms you will handle

Tip for the lab: run a fingertip-light look along the edges. Sharp, straight edges mean little dissolution; soft, curved edges mean a dodecahedral form.
Reality check

A crystal is never perfect

Real crystals are always more or less distorted: stretched, flattened or lopsided, because carbon did not reach every face equally while they grew.

The planner works with the stone in front of them, not the textbook shape. Learning to see the ideal form inside a distorted crystal is today’s key skill.
Combination forms
Octahedron + cubeDrag to turn

Two kinds of faces on one crystal

When conditions change while a crystal grows, it can carry both cubic and octahedral faces at the same time.

Octahedral faces dominate; small square cube faces cut the corners.

Combinations can lead to complex crystals

You do not need to name every complex form. What matters is recognising that a crystal has more than one set of faces.

Section 04 · Polycrystals

Twins & aggregates

Not every rough diamond is one crystal. Twins are two crystals grown together by a fixed rule. Aggregates are many small crystals grown together in no order at all.

Twin 1 · the macle
MacleDrag to turn

The macle: a flat triangular twin

Two halves of an octahedron share one face, with one half turned through 180°. The twin grows flat and triangular.

How to spot one: a flat triangle, often with a herringbone “twin line” running along the edge where the two halves meet.
Macles in the rough parcel

Flat, triangular, and easy to recognise once you have seen a few

Twin 3 · irregular penetration twins

No single rule, many crystals

Several octahedra grown through one another at irregular angles. These stones are hard to plan because the grain changes direction inside them.

Aggregates · tap each card

Many tiny crystals, grown together

Ballas TAP ↻

Ballas, or spherulite

A ball-shaped aggregate with a radial, needle-like structure. Very tough, because there is no single cleavage direction to split along.

Boart TAP ↻

Boart (bort)

Aggregates of poor, industrial quality. The general trade name for rough that is not good enough for gems; it is crushed for abrasives.

Carbonado TAP ↻

Carbonado

A porous, usually black or dark grey variety. When it contains magnetite (and so responds to a magnet) it is called “stewartite”.

Hailstone boart TAP ↻

Hailstone boart

Another aggregate variety, built of concentric layers so it looks like a hailstone.

Section 05 · Natural phenomena

Surface features

The skin of a rough diamond is a diary. Trigons, striations, dissolution marks and coatings tell you how it grew and what it went through.

Four things to look for

Trigons

Triangular etch pits

Striations

Fine parallel lines

Dissolution marks

Layers, steps and holes

Coatings

Skins, frosting, stains

Each type of face etches in its own pattern: triangles on octahedral faces, squares on cube faces, and elongated marks on dodecahedral faces.

Trigons · the signature of the octahedral face

Triangles that point the other way

On an octahedral face the etch figures are small triangles called trigons. They are etched in by the magma, not grown.

Key test: trigons point in the opposite direction to the triangular face they sit on.
Trigons can survive on a natural (a patch of original crystal skin left on a polished stone), a useful clue that the surface is original.
Types of trigon
aPointy trigonbTrigon with a flat base and steep sidescTrigon with a flat base and step-like sidesdTriangular pyramideGroup of pointy trigons (at dislocations)fHexagonal etch figures
Striations

Fine parallel lines across the faces

Dissolution marks

Steps, layers and holes etched by the magma

Plastic deformation: the crystal lattice was bent by stress deep in the Earth. The fine parallel lines it leaves are often seen on brownish stones.
Coatings

A skin that hides the inside

Some crystals carry a translucent or opaque coating. It hides the colour and clarity inside, so the sorter has to look through any clear “windows”, which makes coated rough a gamble.

Two special skins

Frosted

A fine, matt, frosted surface from etching. It scatters light, so the stone looks milky until it is polished.

Radiation stains

Natural radiation in the ground leaves green or brown spots, or a whole green skin. The colour is only skin-deep and is usually lost on polishing.

LAB
Hands-on

Read a rough parcel

Loupe, tweezers and a parcel of rough. Today you are a rough reader: name the form, then find the clues on the skin.

Lab session · suggested flow
  • Pick up each stone with tweezers. Turn it slowly under a 10× loupe with good daylight-type light.
  • Name its form: octahedron, dodecahedron, cube, macle, twin or aggregate. Say if it is distorted.
  • Find an octahedral face and look for trigons. Check they point opposite to the face.
  • Note any striations, dissolution marks, coating, frosting or radiation stains.
  • Record it: stone no. · form · surface features · notes. Sketch one crystal and label its faces.
