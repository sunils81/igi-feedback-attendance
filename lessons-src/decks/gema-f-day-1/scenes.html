Section 01

What is gemmology?

The study of gem materials, from their origins to their use in jewellery and ornamentation.

The gemmologist

Identifies a gem, and determines its nature

Is it

Natural?

Formed entirely by nature.

Is it

Treated?

Natural, but altered by people.

Is it

Synthetic?

Made in a laboratory.

Why it matters: accurate identification is essential to the jewellery trade and to the collector. Price, disclosure and trust all depend on it.
A short history

Two thousand years of looking closely

c. 315 BCETheophrastus

Greek philosopher: On Stones, the oldest surviving work on gems.

c. 77 CEPliny the Elder

Natural History: classified gems, their properties, uses and treatments.

c. 1048Al-Biruni

Persian scholar: measured densities of gems with remarkable accuracy.

1546Agricola

Georgius Agricola, the "father of mineralogy": minerals described by their properties.

Crystals

02
Section 02

Say it precisely

In the exam and in the trade, every word has a meaning. "Gemstone" and "gem material" are not the same thing.

Definition

What is a gem?

A natural or artificial material with a combination of beauty, rarity and durability, used for adornment or ornamentation, especially when cut and polished.

Two terms you must not mix up

Gemstone ≠ gem material

Gemstone
Only for natural minerals, rocks and biogenic materials formed completely by nature, without human interference.
Gem material
The broader, collective term: any substance, natural or artificial, that may be used in jewellery or ornamentation.
So: a synthetic ruby is a gem material, but it is not a gemstone. A glass imitation is a gem material too.
Natural materials

Mineral or rock?

Mineral
A naturally occurring, solid, inorganic material with a crystalline structure, formed by geological processes.
Rock
A naturally formed solid made of an aggregate of minerals: one mineral or several.
More terms

Rough, ornamental and biogenic

Rough

Any gem material in an unfashioned state: not carved, tumbled, cut or polished. Crystals, fragments, pebbles, even synthetic crystals.

Ornamental

Gem materials used for decoration: carvings, vessels, inlays, even architecture.

Biogenic

Produced by the activity of living organisms: pearl, coral, amber, jet, shell, ivory.

Made by people

Artificial is the umbrella. Synthetic and imitation sit under it.

Artificial

Manufactured

Made from natural or artificial components, not formed naturally. Cheaper and easier than mining; produced in quantity. Glass, synthetic ruby.

Synthetic

Has a natural counterpart

Essentially the same composition and atomic structure, so the same physical, chemical and optical properties. In the trade: "laboratory-grown" or "laboratory-created".

Imitation

Looks like, isn't

Natural or artificial material that imitates the appearance of another gem without its properties. Glass for diamond; dyed stone for a costly one.

The trap: because a synthetic has the same properties as the natural stone, most standard tests give the same readings. That is why so much of this course is about inclusions and growth features.
A core Foundation idea

One species, many varieties

A species is defined by its chemical composition and crystal structure. A variety is a distinct colour or appearance within that species. Same tests, same properties; different names in the trade.

Species · beryl
Species · corundum
Exam tip: "Ruby is a variety of corundum." Name the species first when asked to identify a stone: the variety is the colour name.

A century of laboratory gems

1902Flame fusion

Auguste Verneuil announces synthetic ruby, grown as a boule in a flame.

1930s–40sFlux growth

Commercial flux-grown synthetic emerald appears.

1950s–60sHydrothermal · HPHT

Hydrothermal quartz and emerald; GE grows diamond by HPHT (1954).

1976Cubic zirconia

CZ, grown by skull melting, becomes the leading diamond imitation.

TodayCVD diamond

Laboratory-grown diamonds sold in volume worldwide.

Verneuil's method is still used today, largely unchanged. Synthetic ruby from it is used in watch bearings, lasers and jewellery.

Who decides the words?

CIBJO · the World Jewellery Confederation

1926

Founded. The leading international body representing the jewellery, gemstone and precious-metal trades.

45+

Countries represented through national trade associations and commercial bodies.

Blue Books

Its published standards for nomenclature and disclosure: diamonds, gemstones, pearls, coral, precious metals.

The definitions in this chapter follow the same principle as CIBJO: describe a material for what it is, and disclose what has been done to it.

Section 03 · Attributes of a gem

"Beauty, durability and rarity: such are the three cardinal virtues of a perfect gemstone."

Dr G. F. Herbert Smith, eminent gemmologist and former President of Gem-A

How the attributes connect

Three virtues create desire. Desire creates value.

And all five depend on one thing: knowing what the gem actually is. That is your job.
Beauty

Personal, cultural, and built from five things you can observe

  • Colour
  • Lustre
  • Transparency
  • Style of cut and shape
  • Optical effects

Chapter 2 (Days 3–5) teaches you to observe and describe each of these.

Durability

The longer a gem resists damage, the longer it keeps its beauty and value

Hardness

Resistance to scratching and abrasion. Sapphire (9) shrugs off dust; softer stones dull.

Toughness

Resistance to chipping and breaking. Jade is tough enough to carve thin; it is not especially hard.

Stability

Resistance to change from heat, chemicals or UV light. Peridot is attacked by acids.

Hard is not the same as tough. Diamond is the hardest natural material, yet a sharp knock along its cleavage can split it. Chapter 4 (Days 10–11).
Rarity and desirability

Rare in many ways

Geographic: one sourceRare coloursLarge sizesHigh qualityUnusual optical properties

What drives desire

  • Fashion trends and cultural taste
  • Royalty, aristocracy or celebrity
  • Locality: where it was found
  • Myths, legends and birthstones
  • Ethics: sustainability, labour, traceability
