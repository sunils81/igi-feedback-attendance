Section 01

Hardness and the Mohs scale

Hardness is a material's resistance to scratching. It decides how long a polished surface stays sharp.

Friedrich Mohs · 1812 · a relative scale

Ten minerals, in order

HMineralHMineral
1Talc6Orthoclase feldspar
2Gypsum7Quartz
3Calcite8Topaz
4Fluorite9Corundum
5Apatite10Diamond
Each mineral scratches those below it. Everyday comparisons: fingernail 2½ · copper coin 3 · glass 5½ · steel blade 6½ · hard steel file 7½.
The steps are not equal

The gap from 9 to 10 is huge

  • Mohs is an order, not a measurement.
  • Diamond (10) is many times harder than corundum (9).
  • The gap between 9 and 10 is larger than from 1 to 9.
Why 7 matters: household dust contains quartz (H 7). Gems below 7 slowly lose their polish and their facet edges become rounded.
Should you test it?

A destructive test

  • Hardness points or pencils scratch the specimen: never on a cut gem.
  • At most, used on rough or an inconspicuous spot of a carving, by experienced hands.
  • Gemmologists use non-destructive tests instead (RI, SG, magnification).
Use it as evidence: rounded facet edges and scratches tell you a stone is relatively soft (Day 4).
Hardness can depend on direction

Directional hardness

Kyanite

The extreme example

About 4½–5 along the length of the crystal, about 6½–7 across it.

Diamond

Used by cutters

Some directions are harder than others: diamond is polished with diamond powder along the "softer" directions.

Cut gems

Polish quality

Different facets of one stone can take a slightly different polish for the same reason.

Directional hardness follows from the crystal structure: atoms are bonded more strongly in some directions (Chapter 3).

Toughness: resistance to breaking

A hard gem is not necessarily a tough one. Toughness depends on structure, cleavage and inclusions.

Toughness

From poor to exceptional

ToughnessExamples
ExceptionalNephrite (felted fibres), jadeite (interlocking grains)
GoodCorundum, diamond (but it cleaves), chalcedony
FairBeryl, quartz, garnet, spinel
PoorTopaz (cleavage), opal, peridot, zircon (brittle), fluorite
Why jade is so tough: it is an aggregate of tiny interlocking crystals or fibres; a crack cannot run straight through.
Fracture · breakage in any direction, not along a plane

Types of fracture

Conchoidal
Shell-like curved surfaces

Conchoidal

Glass, quartz, obsidian: curved ridges like a shell.

Hackly / splintery
Jagged or fibrous

Hackly · splintery

Fibrous and aggregate materials such as nephrite.

Uneven
Rough and irregular

Uneven

Granular or irregular surfaces.

Incipient
Inside the stone

Incipient fracture

A crack that has started but not broken through: a weakness.

Cleavage: breaking along planes

Some gems split cleanly along planes of weak atomic bonding. A cutter must know where they are.

Cleavage

Directions and quality

GemCleavage
Diamond4 directions, octahedral · perfect but difficult
Fluorite4 directions, octahedral · perfect, easy
Topaz1 direction, basal · perfect
Calcite3 directions, rhombohedral · perfect
PeridotImperfect
ZirconPoor / indistinct
Described by number of directions and quality: perfect, good, poor, indistinct.
In cut stones

Cleavage in practice

Inside

Incipient cleavage

Flat, mirror-like planes, often with iridescence; may be stepped.

Surface

Cleavage vs fracture

Cleavage is flat and stepped; fracture is curved or irregular.

Trade

Cutters use it

Diamond was traditionally cleaved to divide rough; topaz is oriented to avoid a table parallel to the cleavage.

Did you know?
Mohs

A mineralogist, not a jeweller

Friedrich Mohs devised his scale in 1812 for identifying minerals in the field.

Diamond

Hard, but it chips

A sharp blow along a cleavage plane can split a diamond; girdles and points are vulnerable.

Jade tools

Tough enough for axes

Nephrite and jadeite were used for tools and weapons for thousands of years.
