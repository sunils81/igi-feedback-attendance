Section 01

Measuring how light bends

Light slows down and bends as it enters a gem. How much it slows is the refractive index.

Refractive index and birefringence

RI: a number for each gem

  • RI compares the speed of light in air with its speed in the gem.
  • Singly refractive (cubic, amorphous): one RI in all directions.
  • Doubly refractive (all other systems): the light splits into two rays with two RIs.
  • Birefringence = highest RI − lowest RI.
Examples: quartz 1.544–1.553 (0.009) · corundum 1.762–1.770 (0.008) · spinel 1.718 (SR).
Step by step · flat facet method

Six steps

  • Clean the stone and the refractometer glass (hemicylinder).
  • Put a tiny drop of contact liquid (RI about 1.79–1.81) on the glass.
  • Place the table facet gently on the drop.
  • Use a yellow (monochromatic) light; look through the eyepiece.
  • Read where the shadow edge meets the scale.
  • Rotate the stone and read again: does the edge move?
What the scale tells you

Four kinds of result

One edge, no change

Singly refractive

One reading as you rotate: e.g. spinel 1.718, garnet.

Two edges

Doubly refractive

Two readings; record the highest and lowest on rotation. Birefringence = difference.

No edge on the scale

Over the limit

RI above the contact liquid (about 1.79): e.g. zircon, diamond, demantoid.

Vague "spot" reading

Cabochon

Curved stones: the distant-vision (spot) method gives an approximate RI.

Safety: the contact liquid is toxic: use the smallest drop, wipe it off at once, wash your hands. Never scratch the soft glass with the stone.

Light in one plane

Two polarising filters, crossed, show how a gem treats polarised light.

The polariscope

Crossed filters, then rotate

  • Turn the top filter until the field is darkest (crossed position).
  • Place the stone on the glass between the filters.
  • Rotate the stone slowly through 360°.
  • Watch: does it stay dark, blink, or stay light?
Works on transparent and translucent stones; it says nothing about opaque ones.
What you see on rotation

SR, DR, AGG, ADR

ReactionOn rotating 360°MeaningExamples
SRStays darkSingly refractiveSpinel, garnet, glass, diamond
DRLight and dark four timesDoubly refractive (single crystal)Quartz, corundum, tourmaline
AGGStays lightAggregate (polycrystalline)Chalcedony, jadeite, nephrite
ADRIrregular patches, bands or "tabby" patternAnomalous double refraction: strain in an SR materialGlass, synthetic spinel, some garnets, diamond
ADR can fool you: a strained singly refractive stone may brighten. The pattern is patchy and irregular, not four clean blinks.

The conoscope: interference figures

How

Find the optic axis

For a DR stone, find a direction where it stays dark on rotation; place the conoscope sphere above it.

Uniaxial

A cross

Tetragonal, trigonal, hexagonal gems: a dark cross with coloured rings (e.g. quartz shows a "bull's-eye").

Biaxial

One or two brushes

Orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic gems: curved dark brushes.

For Foundation: know that the figure confirms DR and indicates uniaxial or biaxial. More in Block 2.

Describe how a polariscope is used to decide whether a transparent gem is singly refractive, doubly refractive or an aggregate.

Method, then the three results.

1Set the filters to the crossed (dark) position.
1Place the stone between the filters and rotate it through 360°.
1SR: stays dark throughout (beware ADR: irregular patches).
1DR: becomes light and dark four times.
1AGG: stays light throughout.
