Good habits from day one

Cleaning and handling

  • Clean first with the black gem cloth. Fingerprints, oil and dust distort the surface and can be mistaken for inclusions.
  • Use tweezers, holding the stone gently by the girdle. Fingers leave grease.
  • Work over a tray or cloth, low to the bench: a dropped stone chips or disappears.
  • Do not over-grip. Squeezing makes stones fly; soft or cleavable stones can be damaged.
  • One stone at a time, back into its labelled paper before you take the next.

Nomenclature and disclosure · vote, then click

On a sales tag: fair or misleading?

"Laboratory-grown ruby"Synthetic ruby, labelled like this
Fair

Accepted term

"Laboratory-grown" and "laboratory-created" clearly declare a synthetic.

"Genuine synthetic emerald"A synthetic emerald
Misleading

Mixed message

Words like "genuine", "real" or "natural" must not be used with synthetics.

"Sapphire, heated"A heat-treated natural sapphire
Fair

Disclosed

The treatment is stated. Disclosure is what keeps trust in the trade.

"Gold topaz"A yellow quartz (citrine)
Misleading

Wrong species

It is quartz, not topaz. Call it citrine.

"Cultured pearl"A pearl grown with human help
Fair

Accepted term

"Cultured" declares human intervention in the pearl's growth.

"Diamond"Cubic zirconia in a ring
Misleading

An imitation

CZ must be named as cubic zirconia, never as diamond.

What moves desirability today

Stories and ethics both change value

Myths, legends and history

Birthstones, astrological stones and royal histories make people want particular gems, and pay more for them.

Ethics

Customers increasingly ask: where was it mined, by whom, and can you trace it? Responsible sourcing, sustainability and traceability now affect value.

Supply and demand: as either changes over time, so does value. Accurate identification stays the foundation.
