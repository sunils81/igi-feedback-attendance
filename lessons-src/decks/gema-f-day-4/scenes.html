The most common inclusion

Partially healed fractures: "feathers"

A fracture partly heals as conditions change during or after growth. Fluid or solid trapped in it leaves patterns with names:

  • Fingerprints in corundum and spinel
  • Tiger stripes in amethyst
  • Trichites in tourmaline
  • Veils in flux-melt synthetic emerald
Found in natural and synthetic stones, and can be induced by treatment. Remember the orientation puzzle.

Section 03

Magnification

Always in this order: unaided eye, 10× loupe, microscope.

The gemmologist's 10× loupe

Why a triplet?

  • Small, portable, usable anywhere: loose or mounted, rough or fashioned.
  • Three lens components: a triplet. It is aplanatic (no distortion of shape) and achromatic (no colour fringes).
  • A doublet is only aplanatic. Stronger loupes exist, but their field of view is small and hard to use.
Technique

Eye · lens · object, at fixed distances

  • Loupe close to the eye; brace your hands together for stability.
  • Move the stone, not the loupe, to focus: surface first, then inside.
  • Surface features: reflected light, stone beneath the light. Internal features: stone at the edge of the cone of light.
  • Turn the stone; vary the lighting; look from every direction.
Same stone, different view

Change the light, and new features appear

The gemmological (stereo binocular) microscope · both eyes at once

Three ways to light the stone

Brightfield

Light straight up through the stone: internal features of transparent and translucent gems.

Darkfield · most used

A shield blocks direct light; inclusions glow against a dark background.

Top illumination

Light from above: surface features, polish, scratches.

Exam favourite · compare them
