Section 01

Atoms and elements

Every property you will test, from hardness to RI, starts with which atoms and how they are bonded.

The atom

Tiny, and almost all empty space

  • All substances are made of atoms, about 0.1 nm wide (ten million side by side make a millimetre).
  • An atom has a nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of electrons.
  • The number of protons defines which element it is: 6 for carbon, 8 for oxygen, 13 for aluminium.
  • An atom is the smallest unit of an element that keeps its chemical properties.
  • An element is a substance made entirely of chemically identical atoms. About 92 occur naturally on Earth.
Earth's crust by weight · these eight make up about 99%

So what? With so much oxygen and silicon around, it is no surprise that most gems are silicates, and that quartz (SiO₂) is everywhere, including in household dust. That is why hardness 7 matters (Day 10).
From atoms to materials

Atoms bond; compounds form

  • Electrons act as the links that bond atoms together.
  • Diamond is the only gemstone made of a single element: carbon bonded to carbon.
  • Every other gem is a chemical compound: two or more different elements chemically bonded.
  • A chemical formula shows which elements and in what proportion. Corundum, Al₂O₃: 2 aluminium to 3 oxygen.

Graphite (pencil "lead") is also pure carbon. Same element, different bonding: one is the hardest natural material, the other leaves marks on paper.

Oxides, silicates, carbonates

Three families cover most of the gems on the Foundation syllabus.

Chemical classification

Silicates · silicon + oxygen + metal

Beryl, peridot, garnet…

The vast majority of gems. Beryl Be₃Al₂Si₆O₁₈; peridot (Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄.

Carbonates · carbon + oxygen + metal

Pearl, malachite

Pearl mainly CaCO₃; malachite a copper carbonate. Attacked by acids.

Note: quartz (SiO₂) is silica: silicon and oxygen without another metal. Diamond (C) is a native element.

Section 03

Untidy or tidy?

Atoms can bond in a disorderly jumble or a regularly repeating framework. It changes everything.

Two kinds of solid
Amorphous · "without form"

Atoms bonded in a disorderly framework. Examples: glass (natural and artificial), amber, opal. No directional properties.

Crystalline

A regular, repeating, three-dimensional arrangement of atoms: a crystal structure. Has directional properties.

Why it matters in testing: directional properties (double refraction, pleochroism, cleavage, directional hardness) only occur in crystalline materials. Glass never shows them.
It is the structure, not the shape, that makes a crystal

Single crystals come in any shape

  • Well-formed: geometric, with flat crystal faces where growth stopped.
  • Broken pieces, water-worn pebbles or faceted gems are still single crystals inside.
  • Single crystals of the same material test the same, whatever their outer shape.
  • Macrocrystalline: crystals large enough to see with the unaided eye.
Many crystals, one material

Polycrystalline, microcrystalline, cryptocrystalline

Polycrystalline

Many crystal grains in different orientations; irregular outer shape.

Microcrystalline

Grains visible only with magnification: e.g. jadeite, lapis lazuli.

Cryptocrystalline

Grains too fine to see even with a standard microscope: e.g. chalcedony, agate.

Interlocking grains make polycrystalline materials very tough: nephrite, the toughest gem, is a felt of interwoven fibres. You will see why on Day 10.

Gem of the Day · 6 of 60

Quartz

CompositionSilicon dioxide, SiO₂Crystal systemTrigonal; hexagonal prisms with pointed terminationsHardness7SG2.65 (crystalline quartz)RI1.544 – 1.553 · DR 0.009Two formsMacrocrystalline (amethyst, citrine, rock crystal, smoky, rose) and cryptocrystalline (chalcedony: agate, onyx, carnelian, chrysoprase)

Quartz is piezoelectric: squeezed, it produces a voltage; given a voltage, it vibrates at a precise frequency. Almost every watch and phone keeps time with a tiny quartz crystal.

Theory · 6 marks

Define the terms amorphous and crystalline. Give two examples of each, and state one way they differ in testing.

Definitions, examples, consequence.

1Amorphous: atoms bonded in a disorderly framework, no regular structure.
1Examples: glass (natural or artificial), amber (or opal).
1Crystalline: atoms in a regular, repeating 3D arrangement: a crystal structure.
1Examples: diamond, quartz (any crystalline gem).
2Crystalline materials have directional properties (e.g. double refraction, pleochroism, cleavage); amorphous materials do not.
