Section 01

Order makes shape

Atoms in a crystal repeat in three dimensions. That order decides the crystal's shape, its markings and how it treats light.

From atoms to what you see

Different structures behave differently

  • They interact differently with light: optically directional properties (Chapter 6).
  • They grow as different crystal shapes.
  • They show different face markings.
  • They can be harder in one direction than another, and cleave along planes (Chapter 4).
All of these can be understood through one idea: crystal symmetry.
Crystallographic axes

Reference axes: the crystal's coordinates

Imaginary lines through the crystal along which the regular arrangement of atoms is measured.

1

Number of axes

Three, or four (trigonal and hexagonal).

2

Angles between them

All 90°, some 90°, 120°, or all inclined.

3

Relative lengths

All equal, some equal, or all different.

The c-axis is the vertical axis. In many gems it is the crystal's length and the direction of strongest colour (remember tourmaline).
Symmetry · three elements

How regular is the crystal?

Axis of symmetry

Rotate and repeat

A line about which the crystal looks identical 2, 3, 4 or 6 times in one full turn.

Plane of symmetry

Mirror

Divides the crystal into two mirror-image halves.

Centre of symmetry

Opposites

Every face has an identical, parallel face on the opposite side.

Most symmetrical: cubic. Least: triclinic. Planes and centres are studied at Diploma; for Foundation, focus on the axis of symmetry.

Rectanglelooking down a matchbox
Answer

2-fold

It looks the same twice per turn: every 180°.

Trianglelooking down a tourmaline prism
Answer

3-fold

Every 120°: the trigonal system.

Squarelooking at a cube face
Answer

4-fold

Every 90°: cubic and tetragonal crystals.

Hexagonlooking down a beryl prism
Answer

6-fold

Every 60°: the hexagonal system.

Seven ways to build a crystal

Every crystal belongs to one of seven systems, defined by its axes and its symmetry.

Crystal system

Cubic

AxesThree, at 90°, all equalSymmetryFour 3-fold axes: the most symmetrical
Cubic gems are singly refractive: light behaves the same in every direction. No pleochroism.
Crystal system

Tetragonal

AxesThree at 90°: two equal, c-axis differentSymmetryOne 4-fold axis
Look for a square cross-section and four-sided pyramids at the ends.
Crystal system

Trigonal and hexagonal

AxesFour: three equal at 120° in one plane; c at 90°, different lengthSymmetryTrigonal: one 3-fold axis. Hexagonal: one 6-fold axis
Same axes, different symmetry. Tourmaline's rounded triangular section shows 3-fold symmetry; beryl's six-sided prism shows 6-fold.
Crystal system

Orthorhombic

AxesThree at 90°, all different lengthsSymmetryThree 2-fold axes
Prisms with rhombic or rectangular cross-sections. Topaz often ends in a flat cleavage plane.
Crystal system

Monoclinic and triclinic

AxesMonoclinic: two at 90°, one inclined. Triclinic: all inclined. All different lengthsSymmetryMonoclinic: one 2-fold axis. Triclinic: none
The feldspars span both: orthoclase (moonstone) is monoclinic; microcline and plagioclase (labradorite) are triclinic.

Anchor examples

Symmetry ladder

Most to least

Cubic, hexagonal, tetragonal, trigonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic.
