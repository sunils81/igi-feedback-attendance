Where gems come from

A handful of places supply most of the world’s fine gems, and many leave a fingerprint inside the stone that can point to its origin.

Inclusions are fingerprints · tap to reveal

Name the gem from what’s inside

We meet every one of these again under the microscope. For now: what grew inside a stone can identify it, separate natural from synthetic, and even hint at its origin.

Jagged cavity: crystal, bubble, liquid
Emerald · Colombia

Three-phase inclusion

A salt crystal and a gas bubble in brine, in a jagged cavity. The classic sign of Colombian (and Afghan) emerald.

Golden fibres fanning out
Demantoid garnet · Russia

Horsetail

Chrysotile fibres radiating from a crystal. Demantoid’s RI is too high for the refractometer, so this helps prove it.

A visitor from the past
Amber · Baltic, Dominican Rep.

Trapped insects

Fossil resin can hold insects and plant parts from millions of years ago. Fakes are made too, so test it.

Wavy stripes
Amethyst · quartz

Zebra (tiger) stripes

Wavy, rippled stripes in a purple stone point to amethyst, usually natural.

A disc around a dot
Peridot

Lily pad

A round disc-shaped fracture around a tiny chromite crystal: a classic peridot inclusion.

A crack with many legs
Moonstone · feldspar

Centipede

Short stress cracks along the cleavage, like a centipede’s legs. Typical of moonstone.

Dark flakes
Emerald · Zambia

Biotite mica

Brown-black mica flakes: Zambian emeralds show these instead of Colombian three-phase inclusions.

Curved lines, like a record
Synthetic ruby · flame fusion

Curved growth lines

Natural corundum grows in straight bands. Curved striae mean a lab-grown Verneuil boule.
